CASUARINACEAE
A.T. Nor-Ezzawanis
Forest Research Institute Malaysia, Kepong, Malaysia
R.Br. in Flinders, Voy. Terra Austr. 2 (1814) 571; Hooker f., Fl. Brit. India 5 (1890) 598; Gamble, J. As. Soc. Beng. 75, 4 (1915) 405; Ridley, Fl. Malay Pen. 3 (1924) 371; Hutchinson, Gen. Flow. Pl. 2 (1967) 142, Fam. Flow. Pl. 3 (1973) 244; Backer & Bakhuizen f., Fl. Java 2 (1965) 8; Keng, Order Fam. Malay. Seed Pl., 3rd ed. (1983) 129; Ng, Tr. Fl. Malaya 3 (1978) 45; Phengklai, Fl. Thailand 2 (1981) 400; Corner, Wayside Tr. Malaya, 3rd ed. (1988) 207; Wilson & Johnson, Fl. Australia 3 (1989) 100; Johnson & Wilson, Fam. Gen. Vasc. Pl. 2 (1993) 237; Runi, Tr. Fl. Sabah & Sarawak 2 (1996) 119; Jury in Heywood et al., Flow. Pl. Fam. World (2007) 91; Mabberley, Pl. Book, 3rd ed. (2008) 159.
Trees (elsewhere shrubs, e.g., Allocasuarina and Gymnostoma), dioecious or less commonly monoecious. Twigs of two kinds: brown stout twigs, green only at the tips, soon becoming woody and developing into branches; and green needle-like twigs with pale swollen joints, borne on the woody twigs, branched or unbranched, deciduous, not thickened nor becoming woody, internodes round and finely grooved or angled. Leaves arranged in whorls of 4–20, reduced to minute teeth in a sheath surrounding the nodes of the green needle-like twigs, apex rounded-acute to narrowly acute. Male inflorescences usually terminal on the green needle-like twigs, simple or compound, erect, spike-like, flowers arranged in whorls, subtended by bracts. Female inflorescence a globose or ovoid head, terminal or subterminal on both brown and green twigs, flowers arranged in whorls, each subtended by a bract. Male flowers: bracteoles 2, small, scale-like, perianth absent; stamen 1, filaments long with small, basifixed anthers. Female flowers: bracteoles 2, perianth absent; ovary small with two long stigmatic arms. Infructescence a globose or ellipsoid cone-like structure comprising alternate whorls of woody bracts and bracteole-pairs with each of the bracteole-pair tightly enclosing one fruit; at maturity the woody bracteole-pairs gaping open to release the fruit. Fruit a samaroid nut with a pair of membranous wings. Seed one per fruit, without endosperm.
Distribution. A southern hemisphere family with 4 genera and 95 species, largely Australian, distributed to the SW Pacific and throughout Malesia to SE Asia (Mabberley, (2008). In Peninsular Malaysia, one genus, Casuarina, with one native species, C. equisetifolia. Casuarina junghuhniana Miq., Gymnostoma nobile (Whitmore) L.A.S.Johnson and G. sumatranum (Jungh. ex de Vriese) L.A.S.Johnson are planted as ornamentals (Boo et al., 1001 Gard. Pl. Sing. (2006) 545).
Ecology. The flowers are wind-pollinated. At anthesis, the filaments elongate exposing the anthers, and the stigmatic arms also project out from the subtending bracteoles. The flat, light, winged fruits are wind-dispersed.
Casuarina is confined to sandy beaches in Peninsular Malaysia. Root nodules, containing symbiotic nitrogen-fixing bacteria, enable Casuarina species to grow in such nutrient deficient habitats as sandy beaches (Corner, 1988).

Taxonomy. Among Malaysian families, Casuarinaceae is related to Fagaceae, Juglandaceae and Myricaceae, all of which have spike- or catkin-like infructescences (Jury, 2007).
CASUARINA L.
(Latin, casuarius = from the green twigs resembling feathers of the cassowary)
Amoen. Acad. 4 (1759) 143; Bentham & Hooker f., Gen. Pl. 3 (1880) 402; Backer & Bakhuizen f., Fl. Java 2 (1965) 9; Ng, Tr. Fl. Malaya 3 (1978) 45; Phengklai, Fl. Thailand 2 (1981) 400; Wilson & Johnson, Fl. Australia 3 (1989) 100; Johnson & Wilson, Fam. Gen. Vasc. Pl. 2 (1993) 237; Runi, Tr. Fl. Sabah & Sarawak 2 (1996) 120; Suhardi, PROSEA 5, 3 (1998) 146.
Trees, bole straight, cylindrical; crown initially conical, then spreading with age. Green twigs usually unbranched; internodes terete, 5–15 mm long, furrows deep. Scale leaves 5–18 per node, apex narrowly acute. Male inflorescences simple spikes with very short internodes, each node with many male flowers. Female inflorescences lateral on persistent woody twigs. Male flowers: at anthesis with filaments elongating and exposing the anthers. Female flowers: ovary superior, initially 2-locular but later becoming 1-locular by abortion, ovules 2 per locule but only one developing, style short with two elongated stigmatic arms. Cone-like infructescence with thin bracts in exposed part; bracteoles protruding. Samaroid nut glabrous, dull grey or pale yellow-brown.
Distribution. Seventeen species in SE Asia, Malesia, Melanesia, Polynesia, New Caledonia, and Australia (Wilson & Johnson, 1989).
Ecology. On infertile, sandy or acid soils; coastal or in mountains.

Small to very big tree, 5–50(–70) m tall, 27–100(–200) cm diameter, usually dioecious, rarely monoecious; sometimes with buttresses to 76 cm high; crown symmetrical, sometimes asymmetrical, lowest branches of large trees usually arising at c. 3 m above the ground. Bark usually greyish brown to grey, sometimes pale red or black, finely scaly-fissured to irregularly coarsely scaly-fissured, easily broken; outer bark brown; inner bark usually pinkish, sometimes pale brown to red, fibrous, soft. Sapwood yellowish to yellow, hard. Woody twigs terete, reddish brown to almost black when dry, hairy or glabrous at base, hairy towards the point of attachment of the green twigs, 2–3 mm thick. Green twigs usually unbranched, terete, usually drying greenish yellow, sometimes reddish brown, to 34 cm long; nodes usually drying yellow, sometimes pale brown, internodes to 1.3 cm long, 0.5–1 mm thick, furrows hairy to glabrous. narrowly acute; on woody twigs to 4 mm long, base hairy, apex recurved; on green twigs 0.5–1 mm long, base glabrous, apex erect. Male inflorescences simple elongated spikes to 9 cm long. Female inflorescences ellipsoid, to 6 mm long, 4 mm diameter. Male flowers: bracts 0.8–1 mm long, transparent with white hairs; stamens with filaments 1.2–3 mm long, translucent in life, drying mid-brown, anthers broadly oblong, c. 0.5 mm long, golden brown in life, drying mid-brown. Female flowers with bracts 0.8–1 mm long, creamy white, with white hairs on margin; ovary white, c. 0.5–1 × 0.2 mm, stigmatic arms red, 1.5–5 mm long. Infructescences: globose or ovoid-cylindric, to 2 cm long, 1.5 cm diameter; peduncles 2–20 mm long; bracts obovate, apex acute, c. 4.5 × 2.5 mm; bracteoles obovate, c. 4 × 2 mm, apex acute, outside with short, white hairs. Samaroid nut with the wing pair connate, broadly elliptic, transparent, pale yellow-brown, c. 3.5 cm long, pointed at apex.
Vernacular names. Ru (Malay, preferred name), aru, eru.
Distribution. Widespread from Myanmar and Vietnam, throughout Malesia to N and NE Australia, and thence to Melanesia, and Polynesia. Although commonly planted as an ornamental tree, this species is native on sandy coasts in Kedah (Langkawi), Penang (Muka Head), Perak (Teluk Batik), Selangor (Jugra FR), Negeri Sembilan (Port Dickson), Melaka (Pulau Besar) and is widespread on the East Coast from Terengganu to Johor.
Conservation status. Least concern.
Ecology. Casuarina equisetifolia occurs naturally along tropical and subtropical sandy coastlines. In Peninsular Malaysia it occurs wild as a light-demanding pioneer tree on sandy coasts, on open sand above the high-water mark where it forms a strip of forest (Corner, 1988). The fallen twigs prevent self regeneration, and often seedlings of Syzygium grande (Wight) Walp. become established in the shade of the Casuarina trees, and eventually transform the vegetation into S. grande forest with a narrow strip of Casuarina on the seaward side.

Uses. Mabberley (2008) reported that it produces the 'best firewood in the world'. In Peninsular Malaysia, it is commonly planted as an ornamental tree and is also used for hedges and topiary because it is easily pruned. Being a pioneer seashore tree, it is important as a windbreak for coastal resorts.
