MEMECYLACEAE
M. Hughes

Royal Botanic Garden Edinburgh,
Edinburgh, U.K.

and

Singapore Botanic Gardens,
Singapore

DC., Prodr. 3 (1828) 5; Triana, Trans. Linn. Soc. London 28 (1873) 153; Brummitt & Woodgyer in
Heywood et al., Flow. Pl. Fam. World (2007) 210. Synonyms: Melastomataceae tribe Mourireae
Cogn., in A.DC. & C.DC., Monogr. Phan. 7 (1891) 1112; Melastomataceae subfamily Memecyloideae,
Krasser in Engl. & Prantl, Nat. Pflanzenfam. III (7) (1893) 197.

Shrubs or small trees with hard wood and very soft, thin, smooth or narrowly fissured or
scaly bark. Twigs winged, grooved or terete, with an interpetiolar line. Stipules absent.
Leaves opposite, simple, glabrous, without glands, usually pinnately veined or with
acrodromus venation, main veins conspicuous to obscure, sessile or petiolate, margin
entire. Inflorescences axillary, many-flowered compound bracteate cymes, thyrsoid to
very compressed to umbellate, glabrous or occasionally puberulent. Flowers tetramerous,
bisexual; calyx glabrous, funnel-shaped or campanulate, 4-lobed or truncate, usually ridged
internally; petals 4, small, simple, ovate to triangular, reflexed, often brightly coloured;
stamens 8, often colourful, filaments folded in bud, anthers with an elongated connective
usually with a gland, dehiscing through slits; style filiform, stigma minute, ovary inferior,
unilocular. Fruits a globose or ellipsoid berry with a thin or thick pulpy or gritty pericarp,
containing one to few large seeds, cotyledons wrinkled in the embryo and semicircular when
expanded.

Vernacular names. Nipis kulit, delek (Malay).

Distribution. The family is spread throughout the tropics, containing six genera and c.
430 species (Renner, Nord. J. Bot. (1993) 519). Two genera are represented in Peninsular
Malaysia, Lijndenia (1 species) and Memecylon (32 species).

Ecology. In Malaysia, the family is represented by slow-growing understorey trees or shrubs
conspicuous for their often blue flowers. Memecylon L. is one of the most diverse understorey
genera in freshwater swamp forest (Corner, Gards. Bull. Sing., Suppl. 1 (1978) 197) and in
lowland forest (Kochummen, Tr. Fl. Pasoh For. (1997) 306). The flowers are visited by bees,
although the function of the oil-producing gland on the anther connective remains obscure
(Renner (1993); Nayak & Davidar, Acta Oecol. 36 (2010) 191). Flowering in some species
appears to be sporadic, with only one branch bearing inflorescences at any one time. Several
species (M. cantleyi, M. coeruleum, M. edule, M. ovatum and M. pauciflorum) have been

Taxonomy. Memecylaceae has been included under Melastomataceae by many authors, for
example by Clarke, Fl. Brit. India 2 (1879) 513; King, J. Asiat. Soc. Beng. 69, 2 (1900)
3; Ridley, Fl. Malay Pen. 1 (1922) 810; Corner, Wayside Tr. Malaya, 3rd ed. (1988)
487); Maxwell, Gard. Bull. Sing. 33 (1980) 34. Airy Shaw (in Willis, Dict. Fl. Pl., ed. 7
(1966) 712) recognised Memecylaceae as distinct and transitional between Myrtaceae
and Melastomataceae, and noted the vegetative similarity to Eugenia/Syzygium. Indeed,
Memecylon was included under Myrtaceae by Blume (Bijdr. Fl. Ned. Ind. 17 (1826) 1093).
Based on wood anatomy (Vliet, Blumea 27 (1981) 395) and floral and leaf characters (Renner,
1993), Memecylaceae is considered to be distinct from Melastomataceae: Memecylaceae are
glabrous, have anthers which dehisce through slits rather than pores, a gland on the anther
connective, larger embryos, 1–5 seeds per fruit (many-seeded in Melastomataceae), and large
leaf sclerids (absent in Melastomataceae).
Molecular studies (Clausing & Renner, Am. J. Bot. 88 (2001) 486; Conti et al. Syst. Bot. 22
(1997) 629) have placed Memecylaceae as a well supported sister group to Melastomataceae.
However, the latest angiosperm classification (Stevens, Angiosperm Phylogeny Website,
updated 12 July 2012) does not recognise Memecylaceae, but includes it in Melastomataceae
as subfamily Olisbeoideae Burnett (Outlines Bot. (1835) 725). Generic relationships within
Memecylaceae have been investigated by Stone (Systematic Botany 31 (2006) 107). Both
Memecylon L. and Lijndenia Zoll. & Moritzi were found to be monophyletic.

1. LIJNDENIA Zoll. & Moritzi
(after Baron D.W.J.C. van Lijnden, 1813–1852, Holland)

Nipis kulit (Malay)

Syst. Verz. in Moritzi et al. (1845) 10; Bremer, Nord. J. Bot 2 (1982) 121.

Shrubs or small trees, narrowly fissured and scaly bark; Twigs 2-grooved. Leaves with a
short petiole: venation acrodromus, main veins conspicuous. Inflorescences many-flowered
compound bracteate cymes, very compressed. Flowers: calyx not ridged internally, anthers
with an elongated connective without a gland. Fruits globose.

Distribution. 12 species; Africa and Madagascar, Sri Lanka, Peninsular Malaysia, Java,
Borneo and the Philippines. One species in Peninsular Malaysia.

Taxonomy. Previously considered synonymous with Memecylon, but re-established (Bremer,
1982) on the basis of leaf venation and calyx ridge characters.

Tree to 15 m tall; bole 15–30 cm diameter. Twigs pale brown, sometimes drying blackish,

Notes. The only species in the family in Peninsular Malaysia with acrodromus venation.
When sterile, it can be confused with glabrous Melastomataceae (especially Pternandra,
although the blackish epidermis on the twigs is absent in Lijndenia and Memecylon) but
when fertile Pternandra is easily distinguished by its ornamented calyxes, larger flowers and
many-seeded fruit. Rhodamnia (Myrtaceae) is superficially similar when sterile, but does not
have glabrous leaves.



2. MEMECYLON L.
(Greek, mimaikylon = the fruit of the strawberry tree Arbutus, Ericaceae)

Nipis kulit (Malay)

Sp. Pl. 1 (1753) 349; Blume, Bijdr. Fl. Ned. Ind. 17 (1826) 1093, Mus. Bot. Lugd.-Bat. 1 (1851) 353;
Miquel, Fl. Ned. Ind. 1 (1855) 572; Clarke, Fl. Brit. India 2 (1879) 553; King, J. Asiat. Soc. Beng. 69,
2 (1900) 71; Ridley, Fl. Malay Pen. 1 (1922) 810; Bakhuizen f., Meded. Bot. Mus. Herb. Rijks Univ.
Utrecht 91 (1943) 333; Furtado, Gard. Bull. Sing. 20 (1963) 119; Maxwell, Malay. Nat. J., 34 (1980) 7,
Gard. Bull. Sing. 33 (1980) 31, Tr. Fl. Malaya 4 (1989) 184; Bremer, Opera Bot. 69 (1983) 7; Corner,
Wayside Tr. Malaya, 3rd ed. (1988) 487.

Shrubs or small trees with smooth or narrowly fissured bark. Twigs winged, grooved or
terete. Leaves subsessile or petiolate, pinnately veined, veins conspicuous to obscure.
Inflorescences many-flowered compound bracteate cymes, thyrsoid to umbellate or very
compressed. Flowers: calyx ridged internally, anthers with an elongated connective usually
with a gland. Fruits globose or ellipsoid.

Distribution. Currently 317 species; in tropical Africa and Madagascar, India and Sri Lanka,
throughout Indo-China and Malesia to Australia. In Peninsular Malaysia 32 species are
recognised.

Ecology. Lowland to lower montane forest, some species are characteristic of coastal forest
or rocky or sandy beaches, occasionally on limestone hills or in peat swamp or heath forest.

Uses. The wood is very dense, hard and durable, and used for house posts, tool handles, mortar
pestles, paddles (M. acuminatum) and anchors (the wood is dense enough to sink in water);
many species make good charcoal (Burkill, Econ. Prod. Malay Pen. 2 (1935) 1450). Corner
(1978) noted that the wood of M. garcinioides is ‘hard as iron’. A few species are reported
by orang asli (indigenous peoples) to have minor medicinal use. Memecylon coeruleum, M.
edule, M. ovatum and M. pauciflorum are planted as ornamental shrubs.

Notes. Key characters for identification are leaf size and venation prominence, leaf colour
on drying, prominence of wings or grooves in the twigs, length of the primary peduncle,
and presence/absence of anther glands and the shape of the anther connective (Fig. 2). The
descriptions below were prepared almost exclusively from herbarium material. Memecylon
can easily be separated from the otherwise vegetatively similar Syzygium by the presence of
an interpetiolar line.

1. Memecylon acuminatissimum Blume Figs. 3, 5, Map 2
(Latin, acuminatus = tapering into an extremely narrow point; referring to the leaf apex)

Mus. Bot. Lugd.-Bat. 1 (1851) 360; Bakhuizen f., Meded. Bot. Mus. Herb. Rijks Univ. Utrecht 91
(1943) 350; Furtado, Gard. Bull. Sing. 20 (1963) 119. Type: Korthals s.n., Sumatra (lectotype L,
isolectotype K[2]).

Shrub or small slender tree to 5 m tall, more rarely to 10–12(–25) m high; bole to 30 cm
diameter. Twigs pale brown or brown, terete, internodes 3.5–10 cm apart. Leaves: petioles
6–10 mm long, 1.5–4 mm wide, blackish when dry; lamina somewhat coriaceous, drying
brown or dark brown above, rusty brown below, lanceolate to ovate lanceolate, 8.5–27 × 3.5–
11 cm, base decurrent to (more rarely) truncate in larger leaves, apex distinctly acuminate to
acuminate, acumen extending for 12–20 mm; midrib flat or slightly raised above, prominent
below, drying darker than or concolorous with the lamina; venation visible to indistinct (rarely
obscure) above, prominent to indistinct below, lateral vein c. 12 pairs; marginal vein visible or
indistinct, running more-or-less parallel 1–3 mm from the margin or looping slightly between
the main veins. Inflorescences on leafy branches, compound dichasia with 30–100 flowers,
total length 5–8 cm, glabrous; primary peduncles 1 or 2, 17–32 mm long, secondary axes c.
5, 10–17 mm long, tertiary much reduced. Flowers: pedicel 2–4 mm, slender, conspicuous
bracteoles absent; calyx pink or grey blue, 3–4 mm diameter, initially globose and completely
enclosing the petals, dehiscing somewhat irregularly, becoming truncate, ovary somewhat
distinct; petals white or pale blue; anther connective blue, J-shaped, with a distinct gland;
style caducous. Fruits: stalk 4–5 mm long; berry elliptic, 11–13 × 6–8 mm, green when
immature, cream flushing violet when ripe, blackish when dry, slightly striate or smooth;
calyx remnant raised; areolus 3–4 mm wide.

Distribution. Sumatra and Peninsular Malaysia. In Peninsular Malaysia known from
Selangor, Negeri Sembilan, Terengganu, Pahang and Johor.

Ecology. In primary lowland mixed dipterocarp forest at 50–550(–900) m altitude.

Uses. Fruits are reported to be stupefying (Gadoh KL 1781).

Notes. Previously considered a synonym of M. oleifolium (Maxwell, Gard. Bull. Sing.
33 (1980) 95). However, M. acuminatissimum has much larger lanceolate leaves usually
with more conspicuous venation than M. oleifolium, with a raised or flattened (not sunken)
midrib. The inflorescence is longer and the fruits are more elongate and dry black with a very
prominent and thick calyx remnant; those of M. oleifolium are elliptic-globose and usually
dry paler, sometimes with feint markings, and have a smaller calyx remnant.
The calyptrate calyx is unusual in Memecylon and appears to be variable in M.
acuminatissimum as circumscribed here. It is also characteristic of M. ruptile. The only
other species with a raised mid-vein on the upper lamina surface, M. megacarpum, can be
distinguished by its shorter petiole and more elliptic (not ovate) leaves. Fertile material can
be instantly distinguished as M. megacarpum has much larger, globose fruit which are almost
sessile. Memecylon acuminatissimum has been misapplied to M. beccarianum Cogn. (Bremer,
1983) which is endemic to Borneo. The latter has longer, more slender inflorescences with
persistent bracts, and leaves that dry pale grey-green with a depressed midrib above.


2. Memecylon acuminatum Sm. Fig. 4
(Latin, acuminatus = tapering into a narrow point; referring to the leaf apex)

Cycl. (Rees) (1813) 23; Triana, Trans. Linn. Soc. London 28 (1873) 158; Clarke, Fl. Brit. India 2
(1879) 562; Cogniaux in A.DC. & C.DC., Monogr. Phan. 7 (1891) 1152; King, J. Asiat. Soc. Beng.
69, 2 (1900) 84; Ridley, Fl. Malay Pen. 1 (1922) 817; Maxwell, Gard. Bull. Sing. 33 (1980) 57, Tr. Fl.
Malaya 4 (1989) 187; Corner, Wayside Tr. Malaya 3rd ed. (1988) 488 . Type: Collector unknown, s.n.,
without locality (holotype LINN (herb. Smith)).

Shrub or tree to 10–15 m tall; bole to 15 cm diameter. Twigs brown, terete, internodes 2–3 cm
apart. Leaves: petioles 4–6 mm long, 1 mm wide; lamina papery or thickish when dry, drying
brown above, slightly paler below, or pale brown-yellowish on both sides, elliptic lanceolate,
3.5–8.5 × 1.1–4 cm; base decurrent; apex shortly acuminate or acuminate, acumen extending
